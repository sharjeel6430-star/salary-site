Every workplace pension gets tax relief, but *how* your employer takes your contribution decides whether you also save National Insurance and student loan repayments. Your payslip or pension provider will tell you which method you're on.

## The three methods
| Method | How it works | Saves Income Tax? | Saves NI? | Saves student loan? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Salary sacrifice** | You agree to a lower salary; your employer pays the difference into your pension | Yes | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| **Net pay arrangement** | Contribution is taken from your pay before Income Tax | Yes | No | No |
| **Relief at source** | Contribution is taken after tax; the provider claims 20% back from HMRC and adds it to your pot | Yes (20% automatic; higher rate claimed via Self Assessment) | No | No |

## Example: 5% of £40,000 (basic-rate taxpayer, England)
| Method | Goes into pension | Yearly take-home | Cost to you |
|---|---|---|---|
| No pension | £0 | £32,319.60 | — |
| **Salary sacrifice** | £2,000 | **£30,879.60** | £1,440 |
| Net pay | £2,000 | £30,719.60 | £1,600 |
| Relief at source | £2,000 (you pay £1,600, HMRC adds £400) | £30,719.60 | £1,600 |

Salary sacrifice leaves you **£160 a year better off** here. That's the 8% NI on £2,000. With a Plan 2 student loan, you'd save another 9% (£180), so the gap becomes £340.

## Example: 5% of £60,000 (higher-rate taxpayer)
| Method | Goes into pension | Cost to you |
|---|---|---|
| **Salary sacrifice** | £3,000 | **£1,740** (saves 40% tax + 2% NI) |
| Net pay | £3,000 | £1,800 |
| Relief at source | £3,000 | £2,400 at first, falling to £1,800 *only if you claim* the extra 20% through Self Assessment |

Many higher-rate taxpayers on relief-at-source schemes **never claim the extra relief**. If that's you, you can claim it for the current year and the previous four tax years.

## When salary sacrifice saves even more
- **£60,000–£80,000 with children:** sacrifice reduces your *adjusted net income*, which reduces the High Income Child Benefit Charge. Your effective saving can exceed 50%. See the [Child Benefit calculator](/child-benefit-calculator).
- **£100,000–£125,140:** each pound you sacrifice recovers Personal Allowance, saving an effective 62% (60% tax + 2% NI). It can also keep you under £100,000 for tax-free childcare and funded childcare hours.
- **Employer NI share:** some employers add part or all of their 15% NI saving to your pension. Ask HR whether yours does.

## Downsides to check
- **Your official salary is lower.** That can reduce mortgage affordability calculations, life cover linked to salary, and statutory payments such as Statutory Maternity Pay.
- **Minimum wage floor:** you can't sacrifice pay below the National Minimum Wage.
- **Low earners on net pay:** if you earn under the £12,570 Personal Allowance, a net pay scheme gives no tax relief through your pay. From 2024/25, HMRC makes a separate top-up, the low earner's pension payment, to make up the difference. Relief at source adds 20% automatically.
- **The April 2029 change:** from 6 April 2029, only the first **£2,000 a year** of pension salary sacrifice will be exempt from NI. Contributions above that will still save Income Tax, but not NI.

## Frequently asked questions
**Is salary sacrifice worth it for basic-rate taxpayers?** Usually, yes. You save 28p in every pound (20% tax + 8% NI), or 37p with a Plan 2 or Plan 5 student loan, versus 20p with net pay or relief at source.

**Does salary sacrifice affect my State Pension?** Not as long as your remaining salary stays above the Lower Earnings Limit, so the year still counts as a qualifying year.

**Can I salary sacrifice a bonus?** Many employers allow bonus sacrifice. It's especially valuable if the bonus would push you over £50,270, £60,000 or £100,000.
